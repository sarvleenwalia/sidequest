# Concept

## Promise

Choose a field you are curious about and discover an ambitious, feasible project worth pursuing. Initial scope is project generation only. Mentoring, portfolio assessment, accounts, collaboration, and employability are later questions, not MVP features.

## Audience

Indian teens and young adults exploring their interests, including students without laboratory access or prestigious institutional networks. Projects use software, simulations, public or original digital material, and humanities research. No hardware requirement.

## What makes a good brief

A precise question; a tangible artifact or argued interpretation; an accessible method; a meaningful comparison or treatment of counterevidence; a bounded first milestone; and honest source lineage. Ambition comes from the question and rigor, not grand titles or unnecessary AI.

## Product flow

Select field, experience, time budget, and optional interests. Receive a complete brief. Generate another if the direction does not fit. Download the chosen brief. Every brief exposes its supporting source and uncertainty about feasibility and originality.

## Differentiation hypothesis

Grounded, discipline-aware briefs could be more useful than generic chatbot project lists. This is a hypothesis. Validate with student selection, mentor review, first-milestone completion, and whether students understand the proposed evidence.

## Boundaries

No MIT endorsement, admission promises, guaranteed research novelty, fabricated references, or claims that exceptional student results are typical. Humanities projects may center on interpretation and creative work; they need not be disguised machine-learning tasks.

## Source diversity
MIT is one example, not the quality standard. Sources include independent developer portfolios, open-source software, research across institutions, visual journalism, humanities projects, and student work. Judge question depth, execution, evidence, and feasibility rather than institutional prestige.
